Why Your Smart Child Can't Find Their Shoes

AS
Avery Sage
Parent & Coach
Updated October 2025

Our Experience in a Nutshell

  • We realized our child wasn't being "lazy" or "defiant"; their brain just ran out of gas.
  • We learned that smart kids often have a "Ferrari Engine" (Intellect) with "Bicycle Brakes" (Executive Function).
  • Nagging never worked; building external systems (checklists, launchpads) did.
  • We stopped trying to "fix" their brain and started building ramps for it.

I distinctly remember standing in the hallway, holding a single sneaker, yelling, "How can you do algebra in your head but you can't find your shoes that were ON YOUR FEET five minutes ago?!"

It felt like a personal attack. It felt like they were ignoring me. It felt like they just didn't care.

But here is the truth we finally uncovered: They weren't ignoring me. Their brain was just busy doing something else.

This is the paradox of the Gifted Child: They can debate the ethics of artificial intelligence, but they can't remember to brush their teeth.

The Ferrari Analogy

Think of your child's brain like a Ferrari engine. It's powerful, fast, and high-performance.

But their Executive Function (the part of the brain that steers, brakes, and remembers where the keys are) is like the brakes of a bicycle.

The engine is simply too powerful for the steering system. That’s why they crash.

The Gap Between "Knowing" and "Doing"

We used to think, "If they are smart enough to understand the rule, they are smart enough to follow it."

We were wrong. Knowing what to do (Intellect) and having the mental energy to execute it (Executive Function) live in two completely different parts of the brain.

The Asynchronous Brain

The Engine (Intellect)

Age Equivalent: 15 years old.
Solves complex problems, dreams big, thinks fast.

The Brakes (Executive Function)

Age Equivalent: 6 years old.
Forgets instructions, loses items, acts on impulse.

Why "Try Harder" Doesn't Work

telling a child with weak Executive Function to "just focus" is like telling a near-sighted child to "just squint harder." It’s not a lack of effort; it’s a lack of tools.

We tried punishments. We tried rewards. We tried lectures. None of it found the shoes.

The Strategies That Finally Worked

We stopped trying to change their brain and started changing their environment. We became the "External Hard Drive" for their internal computer.

1. The "Launchpad"

We designated one square foot by the door. Shoes, backpack, and coat go there. If they aren't there, the day doesn't start. We don't look for shoes anymore; we just look at the Launchpad.

2. The "Point of Performance" Cues

We stopped saying "Don't forget your lunch" while they were upstairs (they forgot by the time they hit the stairs). We put a sticky note on the doorknob that said "LUNCH."

3. The "Body Double"

Sometimes, they just need a co-pilot. I sit in the room while they clean. I don't help; I just sit there. My presence acts like a tether that keeps their balloon from floating away.

The Bottom Line

Your child isn't broken, and they aren't lazy. They are simply driving a very fast car with very small brakes.

Once we accepted that, we stopped being angry and started building better roads. And yes, sometimes we still lose a shoe. But now, at least we know why.
