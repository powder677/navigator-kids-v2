The "Dopamine Menu": How to Fix the "I'm Bored" Problem

EC
Evan Calder
Learning Architect
Updated October 2025

Our Experience in a Nutshell

  • "I'm Bored" was rarely about having nothing to do—it was a brain that was painfully under-stimulated.
  • Mundane tasks felt physically painful to our "Ferrari Brain" kids because the dopamine tank was empty.
  • We learned we couldn't rely on willpower to start tasks; we needed "Starter Dopamine" (Appetizers).
  • Creating a physical "Menu" removed the decision paralysis of "What should I do?"

We've all been there. Our child is surrounded by $500 worth of Legos, books, art supplies, and a bicycle. And yet, they are melting into the couch, groaning, "I'm sooooo booooored."

It's infuriating. But we realized it wasn't a character flaw or ungratefulness. It was chemistry.

In our experience, boredom for a high-potential child isn't just a lack of activity. It is a painful physiological state. Their high-performance brains burn energy fast, and when the fuel (dopamine) runs low, the engine sputters. They physically cannot engage.

The "Cheap Dopamine" Trap

Our kids used to fix this by reaching for a screen. Video games and TikTok provide "cheap dopamine"—intense, immediate, and requiring zero effort. The problem? Once they consumed that high-octane fuel, regular life (like homework or cleaning) felt excruciatingly slow by comparison.

Enter: The Dopamine Menu

We stopped trying to "force" motivation and started engineering it. We borrowed the "Dopamine Menu" concept (popularized by ADHD creators like Jessica McCabe) and adapted it for our kids.

The idea is simple: Decision paralysis is real. When a kid is bored (under-stimulated), they can't think of what to do. So we made a physical menu for them to order from.

Our Real-World Menu

🍟

Appetizers

Quick hits: 5 jumping jacks, petting the dog, drinking cold water.

🍔

Entrees

Deep work: Legos, coding project, reading a graphic novel.

🥗

Sides

Add-ons: Audiobooks while cleaning, music while showering.

🍰

Desserts

High reward: Screen time, sweets (Use sparingly or you ruin the appetite).

How We Use It

We literally printed this out and put it on the fridge. When my son says "I'm bored," I don't list options (which he will shoot down). I simply say, "Go order from the menu."

1. The "Appetizer" Trick

If he can't start his homework (an Entree), I don't force him to start. I tell him to pick an Appetizer first. "Go run around the house two times." That burst of movement releases just enough dopamine to get the engine running. Then we tackle the homework.

2. The "Side Dish" Strategy

Chores are boring. That is a fact. We don't pretend they are fun. Instead, we add a Side Dish. "You can listen to your favorite podcast, but ONLY while you are folding laundry." Suddenly, the boring task becomes a vehicle for the fun thing.

Using AI to Build the Menu

Stuck on ideas? We ask our AI: "Create a Dopamine Menu for an 11-year-old boy who likes Minecraft, space, and cats. Give me 5 Appetizers (quick), 5 Entrees (deep play), and 5 Side Dishes (to make chores better)."

The Takeaway

You can't lecture a brain into being interested. But you can feed it. By treating stimulation like a diet—balancing the healthy "entrees" with the easy "desserts"—we stopped fighting the boredom and started managing the energy.
