The Sensory Survival Guide: Turning Down the Volume at School

RW
Rachel Whitcomb
Founder & Parent Advocate
Updated January 2026

Our Experience in a Nutshell

  • The "After School Explosion" (Restraint Collapse) was our biggest clue that the classroom environment was draining them.
  • We stopped asking for "special treatment" and started asking for "tools to help them focus."
  • Small tweaks—like wearing a hat or sitting on a wiggle cushion—made a massive difference in energy levels.
  • The goal isn't to fix the child; it's to reduce the friction in the environment.

For the longest time, I was baffled. The teacher would tell me, "Oh, your child is an angel! They are so quiet and focused." Then, the second the car door closed at pickup, my "angel" would explode. Screaming, crying, kicking the seat, or just dissolving into a puddle of tears.

It’s called Restraint Collapse. My child was spending every ounce of energy just keeping it together in a sensory nightmare. The flickering fluorescent lights, the scraping of chairs, the 25 other breathing bodies—it was like trying to do algebra in the middle of a Las Vegas casino.

We realized that if we wanted to stop the after-school meltdown, we had to lower the sensory load during the school day.

The "Battery" Concept

Imagine your child starts the day with 100% battery. In a typical classroom, the lights drain 10%. The noise drains 20%. The itchy chair drains 10%. By lunch, they are at 15%. Accommodations aren't about "coddling"—they are about plugging the leaks so your child has enough battery left to actually learn math.

The Toolkit That Saved Our School Year

We stopped using the word "Accommodations" because it sounded like legal trouble to the school. We started calling them "Focus Tools." Here is what actually worked for us:

For the Eyes (Visual Overload)

The "Vampire" Rule

We got permission for them to wear a baseball cap or slightly tinted glasses indoors. It blocks the overhead fluorescent flicker that makes their brain tired.

Natural Light Seating

We asked for a seat near the window (natural light) but facing away from the busy hallway. Less visual chaos = more focus.

For the Ears (Auditory Overload)

The "Focus Mode" Headset

They keep noise-canceling headphones in their desk. They don't wear them during lessons, only during independent work time. It signals "I am working" and blocks the hum.

The Exit Pass

A simple laminated card on the desk. If the noise gets too loud, they can flash the card and go to the water fountain for 2 minutes of silence—no questions asked.

For the Body (The Need to Move)

"Heavy Work" Errands

We asked the teacher to make my child the "Runner." Carrying a heavy stack of books to the library is actually calming for a regulated nervous system.

Strategic Seating

A seat at the back or edge of the row. This lets them stand up behind their desk to work without blocking anyone's view.

How to Ask Without Being "That" Parent

I used to go into meetings armed with laws and demands. It made the teachers defensive. Then I shifted my approach to being a partner.

The Script We Used

Instead of saying: "My child needs to wear headphones because they have sensory processing disorder."

Try saying: "I've noticed that when the room gets loud, he shuts down and stops working. We've found at home that if he puts on headphones for 10 minutes, he gets his work done twice as fast. Can we try that here so he doesn't fall behind?"

See the difference? You are solving the teacher's problem (an unengaged student) rather than demanding special treatment.

The Bottom Line

Accommodations are just tools to level the playing field. If a child needed glasses to see the board, we wouldn't say they were "cheating." If your child needs a wiggle cushion to listen to the story, it's the exact same thing.

Don't be afraid to experiment. When you find the right tool, it doesn't just help your child—it brings peace to the whole classroom.
