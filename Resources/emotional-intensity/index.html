Our Experience in a Nutshell

  • If your child falls apart over "small things" (like toast shape), it's because their brain receives the data as a "big thing."
  • We learned that this isn't "drama"—it's high-definition emotional processing (IMAX vs. Standard TV).
  • Telling them to "toughen up" usually just makes them feel lonely in their big feelings.
  • The goal isn't to lower their intensity, but to help them build a bigger container (Bucket) for it.

If you've ever watched your child collapse in grief because their toast was cut into triangles instead of squares, welcome to the club.

For years, friends and family told us, "She's just dramatic" or "She needs to toughen up." And for a while, I believed them. I tried to logic the feelings away. I tried to punish the outbursts. I tried to explain that the toast tasted exactly the same.

But then I learned the truth: My child wasn't acting out. She was just feeling more.

The High-Definition Brain

Think of it like this: Most people watch life on a standard TV. High-intensity kids are watching life in 8K IMAX with surround sound. They catch every micro-expression, every tone shift, and every disappointment in agonizing detail. The "small" things aren't small to them—they are loud.

Why "Calm Down" Backfires

When we tell a high-intensity child to "calm down" or "stop crying," we are essentially telling them that their reality is wrong. To them, the pain of the broken toast is real. It feels like a tragedy. It feels like a betrayal of expectation.

When we dismiss it, they don't stop feeling it. They just start feeling alone with it. And that often makes the screaming louder, because they are desperate to be understood.

What We Learned

You cannot teach a drowning child how to swim. You have to pull them out of the water first. In a meltdown, your job is safety, not lessons.

How We Started Handling "Too Much"

1. The Bucket Strategy

We started viewing our job not as "stopping" the feelings, but as "holding the bucket." When the big feelings vomit out, we don't try to shove them back in. We just hold the bucket. We say, "Wow, that is a huge feeling. I'm right here. Let it all out."

2. Labeling the Intensity

We gave the intensity a name. We call it "The Surge." When it happens, we say, "Oh, The Surge is here. It's really loud today, isn't it?" Separating the child from the feeling helps them observe it rather than drown in it.

3. Recovery, Not Punishment

After a big explosion, these kids are often exhausted (and sometimes ashamed). We stopped punishing the meltdown. Instead, we focus on recovery: low lights, soft blankets, and water. We treat it like they just ran a marathon—because emotionally, they did.

The Takeaway

Your child's intensity is difficult to parent, yes. But it is also the source of their deep empathy, their passion for justice, and their incredible creativity.

We don't want to dim their light. We just want to buy them some sunglasses so the world doesn't hurt their eyes quite so much.
