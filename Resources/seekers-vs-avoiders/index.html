Sensory Seekers vs. Avoiders: Does Your Child Crash into Walls or Hide Under Tables?

AS
Avery Sage
Parent & Advocate
Updated October 2025

Our Experience in a Nutshell

  • We learned that our child wasn't being "wild" or "difficult"—they were trying to regulate a body that felt out of sync.
  • Sensory Seekers are like empty cups with a hole in the bottom—they need constant refilling.
  • Sensory Avoiders are like tiny thimbles—one drop too much and they overflow.
  • Understanding this "Volume Knob" changed how we parented (and lowered our blood pressure).

I used to watch my child literally bounce off the living room walls—crashing into the sofa, tackling the dog, spinning until they fell over—and think, "Why can't they just sit still?"

Five minutes later, we'd be at a birthday party, and that same "wild" child would be hiding under a table, hands over their ears, refusing to come out because the "lights were too loud."

I felt like I was parenting two different kids. Was it behavior? Was it manipulation? Was I just bad at this?

It turns out, it was physics. Specifically, the physics of Sensory Processing.

The Volume Knob Analogy

Imagine every person has a sensory volume knob. For most of us, it's set to 5. We feel things, but we can ignore them.

Seekers have their knob set to 1. They can barely feel anything, so they have to CRASH and BAN to feel grounded.
Avoiders have their knob set to 10. A whisper sounds like a scream, and a tag on a shirt feels like barbed wire.

The Two Sides of the Same Coin

We realized that our child wasn't trying to give us a hard time; they were having a hard time. Their internal GPS was offline.

The Seeker (The Human Bumper Car)

"I need to feel my body to know where I am."

  • Crashes into furniture (or people).
  • Chews on pencils, collars, or sleeves.
  • Loves tight hugs ("Squeeze me harder!").
  • Never seems to get dizzy.

The Avoider (The Raw Nerve)

"The world is attacking me."

  • Hates getting hands messy (glue, mud).
  • Pickiest eater (textures are the enemy).
  • Melts down in crowded places (Target, parties).
  • Covers ears at sudden noises.

Why They Crash (The Seeker)

When my child tackles me, they aren't being aggressive. They are looking for Proprioception (body awareness). Their brain is whispering, "Hey, where is your arm? I can't feel it." So they slam their arm into a wall, and the brain goes, "Ah, there it is. Thanks."

We learned to stop saying "Stop crashing!" and start saying "Do you need some Heavy Work?"

What worked for us: Carrying the heavy laundry basket, "wall push-ups," or being wrapped up tight in a blanket like a burrito.

Why They Hide (The Avoider)

When my child hides under the table, they aren't being antisocial. They are in survival mode. Their nervous system has hit "System Overload."

Forcing them to "join the party" is like pushing someone who is drowning back into the deep end. They need a life raft.

What worked for us: Noise-canceling headphones, creating a "quiet corner" with low light, and giving advance warning before going to loud places.

The Bottom Line

Once we stopped looking at these behaviors as "acting out" and started seeing them as "regulating," the battles stopped.

We stopped trying to fix them, and started trying to fill (or empty) their cup. And amazingly, once their sensory needs were met, the "behavior" problems usually vanished on their own.
