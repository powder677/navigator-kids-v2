New York IEP Laws: The "Field Memo" State

In most states, you look at the education code. In New York, and specifically New York City, policy is often driven by "Field Memos" and legal advisories. For parents navigating the system, these memos are powerful tools that schools hope you haven't read.

Whether you are navigating the massive NYC Department of Education (DOE) or a smaller upstate district, understanding these specific New York mandates—like the "Parent Member" requirement and the "Turning 5" process—is the key to securing services.

The 2015 "Smoking Gun" Memo for 2e Students

If you have a child who is smart but struggling (Twice-Exceptional or "2e"), New York schools often try to deny eligibility. They will say, "He has a high IQ, so he can't have a learning disability."

This is false, and New York State has admitted it in writing.

The Document: NYSED Field Advisory (June 2015)

Subject: "Students with Disabilities with High Cognition".

What It Says: This memo explicitly forbids districts from denying eligibility based on high cognitive scores. It states that a student can have a "Specific Learning Disability" even with a high IQ.

Your Strategy: Do not just argue; show them the proof. Print this memo. Highlight Paragraph 2. When the Committee on Special Education (CSE) claims your child is "too smart" for services, slide this document across the table.

The NYC "Turning 5" Process

If you are in NYC, the transition from Preschool Special Education (CPSE) to School-Age Special Education (CSE) is known as "Turning 5." It is a critical juncture where many services are often cut.

1. The "Deferral" Option

For children born late in the year (typically July–December), you may be legally able to "defer" the move to CSE for one year. This allows your child to remain in their supportive preschool setting for an additional year, which is often critical for 2e kids who need more social maturation before entering kindergarten.

2. The "Carter Case" (Private School Funding)

If the NYC DOE cannot offer an appropriate public school placement, you may be eligible for a "Carter Case." This is a legal mechanism where the city pays for tuition at a private Non-Public School (NPS) that specializes in learning disabilities. This usually requires suing the district for tuition reimbursement due to their failure to provide FAPE.

The "Parent Member" Requirement

New York law offers a unique protection that most states do not: the Parent Member.

What it is: You have the right to have another parent of a child with a disability present at your CSE meeting. Their role is to ensure the process remains parent-friendly and to answer your questions.

The "Waiver" Trap

When scheduling your meeting, the district will almost always ask you to sign a form "waiving" the presence of the Parent Member. Do not waive it.

The Parent Member is your witness and your ally. Waiving them makes it easier for the district to steamroll you. If the district cannot find a Parent Member for your meeting, they must reschedule—which puts pressure on them to settle.
