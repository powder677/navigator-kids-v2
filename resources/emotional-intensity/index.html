Emotional Regulation

Living Life at Volume 10: Understanding Emotional Intensity

If your child falls apart over the "small things," it's not drama—it's high-definition emotional processing.

✓ No diagnosis required ✓ Backed by neurodiversity research

If you've ever watched your child collapse in grief because their toast was cut into triangles instead of squares, you know the feeling of confusion. To the adult mind, it’s just toast. To the child, it’s a tragedy.

For years, parents of deep-feeling kids are told, "They are just dramatic" or "They need to toughen up." It is easy to start believing that the behavior is a choice or a discipline issue.

But for many twice-exceptional (2e) and gifted children, the truth is different: They aren't acting out. They are just feeling more.

The High-Definition Brain

Think of it this way: Most people watch life on a standard TV. High-intensity kids are often watching life in 8K IMAX with surround sound. They catch every micro-expression, every tone shift, and every disappointment in agonizing detail. The "small" things aren't small to them—they are loud.

The Volume Knob Analogy

Psychologists often refer to this as "Overexcitability" (a term coined by Kazimierz Dąbrowski). It suggests that some nervous systems are simply wired to react to stimuli more intensely.

It’s not that they lack control; it’s that the input signal is stronger. When we tell a high-intensity child to "calm down" or "stop crying" over a small disappointment, we are essentially telling them that their reality is wrong.

To them, the pain of the broken toast is real. It feels like a betrayal of expectation. When we dismiss it, they don't stop feeling it—they just start feeling alone with it.

Strategies for Handling "Too Much"

You cannot teach a drowning child how to swim. You have to pull them out of the water first. In a meltdown, your job is safety, not lessons. Here are three strategies to help manage the surge:

1. The "Bucket" Strategy

It helps to view your job not as "stopping" the feelings, but as "holding the bucket." When the big feelings vomit out, don't try to shove them back in. Just hold the bucket.

Try saying: "Wow, that is a huge feeling. I'm right here. Let it all out. I've got you."

2. Name "The Surge"

Give the intensity a name (e.g., "The Surge," "The Wave," or "The Storm"). When it happens, you can say: "Oh, The Surge is here. It's really loud today, isn't it?"

Separating the child from the feeling helps them observe it rather than drown in it. It reminds them that they are not the problem; the feeling is the temporary problem.

3. Recovery, Not Punishment

After a big explosion, these kids are often physically exhausted and emotionally ashamed. Punishing a meltdown often increases the anxiety that caused it.

Instead, focus on recovery: low lights, soft blankets, and hydration. Treat it like they just ran a marathon—because emotionally, they did.

The Bottom Line

Your child's intensity is difficult to parent, yes. But it is also often the source of their deep empathy, their passion for justice, and their incredible creativity.

We don't want to dim their light. We just want to buy them some sunglasses so the world doesn't hurt their eyes quite so much.
