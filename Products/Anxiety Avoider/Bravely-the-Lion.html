🎯 Skills Practiced

1. Cognitive Defusion (Worry Shrink Ray)

Anxious children catastrophize - they see worries as enormous and insurmountable. Asking "How big is this REALLY?" creates cognitive distance and teaches realistic threat assessment. This is a core CBT skill.

2. Gradual Exposure (Brave Baby Steps)

Avoidance maintains anxiety. The "exposure ladder" breaks scary things into tiny, manageable steps. Each successful small step builds confidence and proves to the brain that it CAN cope. This is the gold standard anxiety treatment.

3. Cognitive Reframing (What If Flip)

Anxious brains default to negative predictions. "What if it goes okay?" teaches children to generate alternative (realistic) outcomes. Over time, this builds a habit of balanced thinking rather than automatic catastrophizing.

4. Effort-Based Self-Worth (Bravery Badge)

Anxious children often avoid because they fear failure. Celebrating TRYING (regardless of outcome) separates self-worth from results. This reduces the stakes of attempting new things.

💬 Conversation Starters
  • "How big is that worry REALLY? Shrink ray time!"
  • "What's the tiniest brave step you could take?"
  • "Let's flip that - what if it goes okay?"
  • "You get a Bravery Badge just for trying!"
🏠 Practice Ideas
  • Shrink ray game: Rate worries on a 1-10 scale together
  • Baby step planning: Before scary events, map out tiny steps
  • What If jar: Write down "what ifs" and flip them together
  • Bravery journal: Note times they TRIED (outcome doesn't matter)
  • Model your own: Share when YOU feel scared and use the tools
⚠️ Important: Avoidance Looks Like Defiance

When anxious children say "NO!" or refuse to do things, it often looks like defiance. But the root is usually fear, not willfulness. Key differences:

  • Defiance: "I won't" (power/control)
  • Anxiety: "I can't" (fear/overwhelm)

If your child avoids things they "should" be able to do, consider anxiety as the cause. Punishment makes anxiety worse; graduated exposure and support help.

⚠️ When to Seek More Support

These tools help mild-moderate anxiety. Seek professional support if:

  • Anxiety significantly impacts school, friendships, or family life
  • Your child has panic attacks or physical symptoms
  • Avoidance is increasing over time despite support
  • Your child expresses hopelessness or excessive worry about safety
