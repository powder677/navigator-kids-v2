Texas ARD & IEP Laws: The "State Plan" is Your Weapon

Most advocates look at federal law (IDEA). But in Texas, smart parents look at the Texas State Plan and the Texas Education Code.

Texas operates differently than other states. We call IEP meetings "ARD" meetings. We have specific statutes for recording meetings. And we have unique mandates for Gifted/Talented (2e) students that most schools try to ignore.

Here are the critical state-specific laws you need to know to dominate your next ARD meeting.

The "Hidden" Mandate for 2e Students: Section 4.9A

If your child is "Twice Exceptional" (Gifted + Disability), schools often refuse to modify the curriculum. They say, "We can't change the curriculum just because he's bored."

They are wrong.

The Source: Texas State Plan for the Education of Gifted/Talented Students

The Mandate: Section 4.9A explicitly requires that curriculum must be modified for gifted students.

The Strategy: When the ARD committee denies modifications, pull out Section 4.9A. It is not a favor you are asking for; it is a state requirement. If they are not modifying the depth and complexity of the work, they are violating the State Plan.

Recording Laws: The "Legal but Banned" Gray Area

Can you record your ARD meeting? This is one of the most common questions from Texas parents.

State Law: One-Party Consent

Under Texas Penal Code § 16.02, Texas is a "One-Party Consent" state. Legally, you do not need the other person's permission to record a conversation you are actively participating in.

The Local Policy Trap

While state law allows recording, many school boards (like HISD or DISD) have specific local policies prohibiting recording on school property. While they likely cannot prosecute you (due to state law), they can end the meeting or ban you from campus if you violate board policy.

The Safe Move: Notify them. Say, "I am recording this ARD for my own records to ensure I capture our agreement accurately." Most districts will simply pull out their own recorder and record you back.

The "Dyslexia 504" Pipeline

Texas schools historically push Dyslexic and ADHD students into Section 504 plans to avoid Special Education paperwork. This was such a systemic issue that the federal government sued the state over it.

The Difference:

  • A 504 Plan offers Access (e.g., audiobooks, extra time).
  • An IEP offers Instruction (e.g., a Certified Academic Language Therapist teaching them to read).

If your child needs to be taught how to read, a 504 is insufficient. You must demand an IEP based on "Educational Need".
