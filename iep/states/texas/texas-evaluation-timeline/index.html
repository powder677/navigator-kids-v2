Texas 60-Day IEP Evaluation Timeline: What Happens When Schools Are Late (2025)

You requested support for your child months ago. The school assured you they would evaluate your child soon. Now, weeks have passed, and you are met with silence while your child continues to struggle without necessary services.

You are not imagining things. They are breaking the law.

In Texas, schools have 60 calendar days to complete IEP evaluations. Not "sometime this semester." Not "when the psychologist is free." 60 days from the date they receive your written consent.

This guide explains the specific legal timelines under the Texas Education Code and provides a procedural roadmap for parents to secure the evaluation their child is entitled to.

The Legal Reality: The 60-Day Rule

Texas Education Code §29.004 requires schools to complete IEP evaluations within 60 calendar days of receiving your written consent.

Important Distinction: This clock runs on calendar days, not school days. This means weekends, holidays, and summer break often count toward the total (unless specific exceptions apply). Many schools will try to tell you otherwise to buy more time.

1. The Response (15 School Days)

From the moment the district receives your written request for an evaluation, they have 15 school days to respond. They must provide one of two things:

  • A formal consent form to begin testing, OR
  • A "Prior Written Notice" (PWN) legally explaining why they are refusing the evaluation.
Note: Verbal requests do not start this legal clock. If you requested an evaluation in a conversation or parent-teacher conference, the clock never started. You must submit your request in writing.

2. The Evaluation (60 Calendar Days)

Once you sign that consent form, the "60-Day Clock" begins. The district must complete the Full Individual and Initial Evaluation (FIIE) and produce a report within 60 calendar days.

3. The ARD Meeting (30 Calendar Days)

Once the evaluation report is completed, the Admission, Review, and Dismissal (ARD) committee must meet to discuss the results within 30 calendar days.

Why Schools Violate the 60-Day Rule

Most Texas schools have a backlog of 40-80 students waiting for evaluations. They are significantly short-staffed on Licensed Specialists in School Psychology (LSSPs).

But that is not your problem to solve—it is theirs. Administrative convenience is never a legal justification for denying a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE).

What This Delay Means for Your Child

Every day without an IEP is a day without the services your child legally qualifies for. If your child needs speech therapy, they are not getting it. If they need a smaller classroom, they don't have it.

Under the law, if a timeline violation denies your child educational benefit, the district may be required to provide "compensatory services" to make up for the lost time.

Your Action Plan: Handling a Late Evaluation

Step 1: Calculate Your Exact Deadline

Do not rely on the school to track the date. Use this formula:

  • Start Date: The day the school received your signed consent form.
  • End Date: Add exactly 60 calendar days.
  • Example: Consent signed September 15 + 60 days = Report due November 14.

Step 2: Formalize Verbal Requests

If your initial request was verbal and ignored, establish a paper trail immediately. Send an email to the principal stating:

"I am writing to formally follow up on my verbal request for an evaluation made on [Date]. Please consider this my formal written request as of today."

Step 3: Send the "Deadline Missed" Notice

If the 60-day window has passed, the district is in violation. Send an email to the Special Education Director immediately:

"My child's evaluation was due on [Date]. It is now [Date]. The district is in violation of TEC §29.004. I expect the report immediately."

Step 4: Request Compensatory Services

If the delay was significant (e.g., 6 days late or 6 months late), request "compensatory services." Texas law favors a qualitative approach—asking for services that put your child in the position they would have been in had the evaluation been timely.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the 60 days include summer break?

This is a common point of confusion. While the general rule is 60 days, if there are fewer than 35 school days left in the year when you sign consent, the report is usually due by June 30th.

What if my child was absent during the evaluation period?

If your child is absent for 3 or more days, the deadline may be extended by the number of days missed. Keep track of attendance records carefully.

Can I request an evaluation even if my child is getting A's and B's?

Yes. Grades are not the only factor. If your child is struggling emotionally or working 3x harder than peers to get those grades, they may still qualify for an IEP.

Will filing a complaint make the school retaliate against my child?

Retaliation is illegal. While schools may become more bureaucratic, filing a complaint often signals that you know your rights, which can actually lead to better compliance.

How long does a TEA investigation take?

If you file a formal state complaint about the delay, the Texas Education Agency (TEA) has 60 days to investigate and issue findings.
