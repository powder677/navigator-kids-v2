Advocacy in the Borderplex

El Paso Independent School District (EPISD) serves as a historic educational pillar in the Borderplex region. With the Franklin Mountains as a backdrop and a vibrant binational culture, EPISD serves nearly 50,000 students. However, the district faces the challenge of declining enrollment and out-migration, leading to ongoing "right-sizing" conversations and school consolidations.

For parents of children with special needs, this instability can create anxiety about the continuity of specialized programs. Whether your child attends Coronado, Franklin, or El Paso High, understanding how to anchor your child's services in the Admission, Review, and Dismissal (ARD) document is critical to weathering administrative changes.

The Texas Autism Supplement in El Paso

If your child is eligible for special education under the category of Autism, Texas law (TAC §89.1055(e)) mandates the consideration of 11 specific strategies. This is not optional.

Why This Matters in EPISD

In a district consolidating resources, specialized staff (like BCBAs or autism specialists) may be stretched across multiple campuses. You must ensure the "Staff-to-Student Ratio" strategy is clearly defined in the IEP. If your child needs a lower ratio to be safe or to learn, the district must provide it, regardless of staffing shortages.

Do not let the ARD committee skip over "Extended School Year (ESY)" services. With the unique school calendar and potential for regression, data collection is key. Learn more about your rights in our Texas Evaluation Timeline Guide.

Strategies for EPISD ARD Meetings

To advocate effectively in El Paso's unique educational environment, consider these three strategies:

1. The Bilingual Assessment Factor

In a border community, distinguishing between a language difference (learning English) and a learning disability is complex. If your child is an English Learner (EL), ensure the Full Individual Evaluation (FIE) was conducted by a qualified bilingual evaluator. If you suspect the evaluation results are skewed by language barriers, you have the right to request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at public expense.

2. Protect Against "Program Moves"

With declining enrollment, EPISD may move specialized units (like ALE or behavioral support classes) from one campus to another. While the district can determine school locations, the ARD committee determines placement. If a move increases your child's commute significantly or disrupts their Least Restrictive Environment (LRE), you can challenge it. Use the TEA Complaint Process if necessary.

3. Document Transitions

For high school students, especially those near graduation, transition planning is vital in the El Paso economy. The IEP should include specific goals related to post-secondary opportunities, whether that's at UTEP, EPCC, or vocational training. Ensure the "Transition Supplement" isn't just a generic form but a real plan.

Navigating the Desert Landscape

Just as hiking the trails of the Franklin Mountains requires preparation and water, navigating the special education system requires a plan and resources. The "Sun City" is known for its warm community, but the bureaucracy of a large, contracting district can feel cold.

Your child's ARD document is their map. It stays with them regardless of which building they are in. By ensuring that services are tied to your child's needs rather than a specific campus's offerings, you provide stability in a changing landscape.
