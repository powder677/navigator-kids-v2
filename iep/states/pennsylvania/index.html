Pennsylvania IEP Laws (Chapter 14): Timelines & Parent Rights

Short answer: Pennsylvania special education is governed by Chapter 14 regulations. Key differences include the NOREP (a mandatory form to finalize decisions), a 60-calendar-day timeline that pauses during summer, and distinct protections for gifted students under Chapter 16.

In plain English: PA gives parents more power than many states via the NOREP form, but also more traps. If you don't object to a NOREP within 10 days, the school can assume you agreed and move forward.

How long does a school have to evaluate in PA?

Short answer: Schools have 60 calendar days (not school days) to complete the Evaluation Report (ER) once you sign the Permission to Evaluate (PTE).

⚠️ The "Summer Stop" Rule

Unlike federal law, PA law pauses the clock the day school ends for summer. The count restarts the day school opens. If you request an evaluation in May, you will likely not get results until October.

What is a NOREP and why is it dangerous?

Short answer: The Notice of Recommended Educational Placement (NOREP) is the document proposing a change (or refusal to change) your child's program. It is the "trigger" for your due process rights.

The Trap: If you receive a NOREP and do nothing for 10 days, the district can implement the changes automatically. You must check "I disapprove" and return it to freeze the current program ("Stay Put").

Can I record an IEP meeting in Pennsylvania?

Short answer: Generally, no. Pennsylvania is a "two-party consent" state (Wiretap Act). Secretly recording is a felony. You must ask permission; if the school refuses, you generally cannot record unless it is an approved ADA accommodation for a parent disability.

Should I sign the "10-Day Waiver"?

Short answer: No. PA law gives you 10 days to review the Evaluation Report before the IEP meeting happens. Schools often ask you to waive this so they can hold the meeting immediately. Keep your 10 days so you can read the report calmly at home.

GIEP vs. IEP: Which one wins?

Short answer: Special Education (Chapter 14/IEP) takes legal precedence over Gifted Education (Chapter 16/GIEP). If your child is "twice-exceptional" (2e), the school will usually combine the goals into one IEP. Ensure the IEP contains a specific section for "Specially Designed Instruction for the Gifted."

What should PA parents do next?

  1. Check your PTE date: Calculate your 60 calendar days, accounting for the summer pause.
  2. Watch for the NOREP: Never let this document sit on the counter. Sign "Disapprove" if you aren't 100% happy.
  3. Download the PA Procedural Safeguards: Search specifically for the "ConsultLine" number (800-879-2301) for free state help.
